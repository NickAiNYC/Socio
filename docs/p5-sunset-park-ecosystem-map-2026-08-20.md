## The baseline picture [VERIFIED]

- Sunset Park has roughly **130,783 residents: 39% Latino, 31% Asian, 24% white, 3% Black.** Nearly half were born outside the US, with major origins including **Mexico**, the Dominican Republic, Central America, China, the Middle East, and Europe. ([Center for Family Life](https://www.centerforfamilylife.org/learn-more-about-our-neighborhood/))
- **18% of residents live below the poverty line; one-third of adults lack a high school diploma.** Relevant to you in two ways: contract literacy varies, and a one-page Spanish agreement is not a nicety — it's the difference between a signature and a stall.
- The neighborhood is **two ethnic economies stacked on the same grid** — a Latino corridor and a Chinese corridor. That split is the single most important structural fact for your targeting.

**That last row is the whole strategic insight** [INFERRED, but strongly supported]: Sunset Park is a labor-supply neighborhood and Park Slope is a demand neighborhood, separated by about fifteen blocks. Your contractors live and buy material in the first and do their highest-value work in the second. **The dollar gap is real:** a documented full gut of a landmarked Park Slope brownstone ran **$1,014,079 for 3,600 sq ft — about $282/sq ft** ([Brick Underground](https://www.brickunderground.com/improve/costs-of-renovating-landmarked-Park-Slope-brownstone-Bolster)). Sunset Park kitchen and bath work is an order of magnitude below that. A contractor who moves from one to the other changes his business permanently — and that framing is a much better pitch than "more leads."

They're a family-services org that happens to sit at the center of the Mexican community's trust network.

## 3. Seasonality

**[INFERRED — NYC construction general knowledge, verify against your own permit pull]**

| Period | Pattern | Your move |
|---|---|---|
| **Mar–Jun** | Ramp-up. Homeowners commit for spring/summer work. Estimates fly. | Peak lead-gen season |
| **Jul–Sep** | Peak execution. **They're too busy to answer your WhatsApp.** | Where you are now. Expect worse reply rates than a spring cohort — don't misread that as a bad script. |
| **Oct–Nov** | Interior work pivot (kitchens, baths, basements). Anxiety about the coming gap begins. | **Your best selling window.** He can see winter coming and his pipeline is thin. |
| **Dec–Feb** | Slow. Cash is tight, crews get furloughed, exteriors stop. | Best window for **dead-lead reactivation** (P3) — he has time to dig out the notebook, and desperately wants work |

**The timing read for you:** you're prospecting in the deadest month of the calendar for contractor attention. Two implications. First, don't over-interpret an August reply rate — a variant that gets 15% in August might get 30% in October. Second, **every conversation you have now should be positioned for the October close.** "Le escribo ahora porque en octubre se le va a poner lenta la cosa, y para entonces ya quiero tenerle obra."

## 5. The customer journey

**[INFERRED — this is a model, hold it loosely]**

**Two distinct journeys run in parallel, and they behave nothing alike:**

**Journey A — the Sunset Park homeowner / small landlord:**
> Problem appears → asks family, neighbors, or the guy at church → gets 1–2 names → calls, maybe visits the job → hires on trust and price → **never searches Google**

Marketing is almost irrelevant here. Referral is the entire funnel. Your value to a contractor working this journey is **speed of response and follow-up on dead estimates** (P3), not lead generation.

**Journey B — the Park Slope / Windsor Terrace brownstone owner:**
> Problem or renovation ambition → Google, Houzz, Block, Instagram → checks reviews and licenses → collects 3 bids → **hires on presentation, insurance, and communication as much as on price**

This is where the money is and where your Sunset Park contractors systematically lose. Not on skill — on **response speed, English-language proposal polish, review count, and visible licensing.**

**The pitch this implies:** you are not selling him leads. You are selling him access to Journey B, which he currently cannot reach. That reframe is worth more than any script in the P1 document — and it's what you should say out loud at the coffee meetings.

## 6. The respected contractors

**[UNKNOWN — and I want to be straight with you: this is not discoverable from the web.]**

The 2–3 contractors everyone respects in Sunset Park are known by word of mouth and nothing else. They may have no website, no Google listing, and a full book of work they'd never admit to a stranger. **How to actually find them — three methods, in order of speed:**

1. **Ask the supply house counter guy.** "¿Quién es el maestro más cabrón que viene aquí?" He knows. He knows because that guy buys the most and pays on time. This is a 30-second question during your Kamco/Feldman visit and it's arguably the highest-value thing you'll get out of that trip.
2. **Ask every contractor you talk to** — including the ones who say no — "¿A quién respeta usted en el barrio?" Names that come up twice are your answer.

That gives you volume, which is a decent proxy for respect — though the most respected guy is often the one who *doesn't* file, so triangulate.

**Why this matters more than it looks:** in a referral-native trade, one respected contractor as a public reference is worth more than fifty cold sends. If your first pilot is someone the neighborhood respects, the second through tenth get much easier. **Consider deliberately over-serving your first signed pilot at a loss** — the case study is the actual product of the pilot phase.

## 7. Three cultural details that build trust fast

**[INFERRED — from general knowledge of Mexican and Central American business culture in NYC. Treat as hypotheses to test, not rules.]**

**1. Usted, always. And "Don" earns its keep.**
Your existing scripts do this correctly. Using `tú` with a 45-year-old maestro you've never met reads as disrespectful or as a marketer who bought a Spanish template. `Don {{firstName}}` — first name, not surname — is the register of respect between working men who don't know each other yet. Never switch to `tú` until he does.

**2. Don't lead with the number. Lead with the person.**
An American cold pitch opens with the value proposition. In this context, opening with a number before establishing who you are and who sent you reads as *someone trying to take something*. The first thirty seconds should establish where you're from and what you saw, not what you charge. All three P1 variants are built this way — the price appears only after the pain is his.

**3. The word "socio" is doing real work, and you should say it out loud.**
The distinction between an *agencia* (takes money, delivers slides) and a *socio* (shares risk, eats the downside) is culturally sharp here. Your entire commercial structure — $0 until his customer's deposit clears — is the literal enactment of that word. **Say it explicitly at the coffee meeting: "No soy agencia. Si usted no cobra, yo no cobro. Por eso se llama Socio."** That's the sentence that ends the anti-agency reflex, and it happens to be completely true, which is why it works.

**A fourth, less comfortable one [INFERRED]:** immigration status anxiety is real and is a live background condition in this community in 2026. Never ask about documentation, never ask for a Social Security number in early conversations, and never require anything that looks like a government-facing form to start. Anything that reads as data collection on behalf of an institution will end the conversation and cost you the referral network behind it. Your $0-upfront, WhatsApp-native, one-page-Spanish-agreement setup is well designed for this — **keep it that way even when it becomes operationally inconvenient.**
